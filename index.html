The Hardwood Floor Installer Problem Nobody Talks About

Here's the thing that took me way too long to understand: the hardwood floor installer industry has almost no standardized accountability. A guy can call himself a "hardwood specialist" and get a business card printed at Staples the same day. The reviews platforms are gamed — I found three contractors whose review profiles had suspicious spikes of five-star reviews in a single week, all with similar phrasing. The licensing requirements in Washington State cover general contractor status, but nothing specifically validates whether someone has actually installed hardwood floors before.

What this means in practice is that the real differentiator isn't skill advertising — every installer claims to be skilled — it's subfloor preparation, and almost nobody talks about it. I watched two installers skip moisture testing entirely. One told me the subfloor "looked fine." A floor that gets installed over a subfloor with elevated moisture readings will cup, crack, and gap within eighteen months. You won't know until it's too late and your warranty argument becomes a he-said-she-said. The installers who test, prep, and document that step are the ones worth your money. Most won't bother unless you specifically ask.

Questions Everyone Keeps Asking

How much does hardwood floor installation cost per square foot in 2026?

In the Auburn, Kent, and Renton area of Washington State, I found a range of $6.80 to $14.80 per square foot for complete installation including materials, subfloor prep, and finishing. The realistic sweet spot for a solid mid-grade hardwood installed properly by a licensed contractor is $9 to $12 per square foot. Anyone significantly below that range without a clear explanation of what's excluded should be questioned carefully.

How do I verify a flooring contractor is actually licensed in Washington State?

Go to the Washington State Department of Labor & Industries contractor lookup tool at lni.wa.gov and search by business name or license number. A valid contractor's license in Washington requires proof of insurance and a bond — both of which protect you if the work is substandard or causes damage. Don't skip this step. Two of the twelve contractors I looked at in this process had lapsed licenses.

What's the difference between solid hardwood and engineered hardwood installation?

Solid hardwood is a single piece of wood throughout — it must be nailed or stapled to a wood subfloor and can't go over concrete or radiant heating systems. Engineered hardwood has a real wood veneer over a plywood or HDF core and can float, glue, or nail down over a wider variety of substrates including concrete. For most Pacific Northwest homes with older subfloors or radiant heat, engineered is often the more practical choice — a good installer will tell you this honestly upfront.

How long does a typical hardwood floor installation take?

For an average residential project between 800 and 1,500 square feet, expect three to five days from crew start to walkthrough. That includes subfloor prep, installation, and finish time if site-finishing is involved. Jobs that seem to go faster than that — especially jobs completed in a single day for large spaces — are usually cutting corners on subfloor prep or acclimation time for the materials.

What should be in a hardwood floor installation contract?

At minimum: scope of work with specific species and grade of material, total square footage, start and completion dates, payment schedule (never pay 100% upfront), what happens if additional subfloor work is needed, and warranty terms for both labor and materials. If a contractor resists putting any of these things in writing, that's your answer about whether to hire them.

How do I know if my subfloor needs work before hardwood installation?

A legitimate installer will test moisture content with a meter before doing anything else — the NWFA recommends hardwood be installed only when subfloor moisture is within 4% of the hardwood's moisture content. They'll also check for flatness: the standard is no more than 3/16 inch variation over a 10-foot span. If an installer looks at your subfloor and immediately says it's fine without testing or measuring, ask them to show you their readings. If they don't have readings, you have your answer.

Is it worth paying more for a contractor who does site-finishing versus prefinished hardwood?

Site-finishing — where the floor is sanded and finished after installation — gives you a more seamless look, especially at seams and transitions, and allows for custom stain colors. Prefinished hardwood is faster, has a factory finish that's often harder than what a site crew can apply, and gets you back in the space sooner. For high-traffic homes with kids or pets, I'd lean prefinished. For a space where aesthetics are the top priority, site-finishing can be worth the extra time and cost.

Can hardwood floors be installed over radiant heating?

Yes, but with important caveats. Solid hardwood thicker than 3/4 inch is generally not recommended over radiant systems because the heat cycling causes too much expansion and contraction. Engineered hardwood in the 3/8 to 1/2 inch range performs much better. The system also needs to be running for several days before installation to stabilize the subfloor moisture, and the floor needs adequate acclimation time in the space. Any installer who doesn't ask about your heating system before quoting a hardwood job is skipping a critical step.
